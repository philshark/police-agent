Family Safety Apps Are Tearing Families Apart
Half of parents use location tracking on their teens. Researchers are starting to ask whether that's actually a good idea.
Sinead
2 min read•June 10, 2026
The pitch was simple. Your family, always connected. Know where everyone is. Peace of mind, all the time.
It sounded reasonable. It still does, in the abstract. Of course parents want to know their children are safe. Of course families want to stay connected. The technology existed, the apps arrived, and tens of millions of families signed up. But something unexpected happened.
The surveillance problem nobody predicted
About half of parents in the US now use location-tracking apps to monitor their teenagers' movements, according to a study published in the Journal of Family Psychology. A further 14% of parents in the study were tracking their children without the child's knowledge - the monitoring was happening without the teen being aware of it at all.
That second statistic is worth sitting with. One in seven families using these apps are doing so secretly. Not as a deliberate act of deception, necessarily - more often as a gradual slide from "checking in" to "checking up" that happens so slowly nobody quite noticed the line being crossed.
When safety becomes surveillance
In 2023, 61% of then-college-aged young people reported their parents had used Life360 when they were in high school. That's a generation of young people who came of age under near-constant parental surveillance and researchers are only beginning to understand what that means for trust, autonomy, and the parent-child relationship.
What they've found so far isn't entirely reassuring.
Here's the paradox at the heart of family tracking: the parents most likely to use surveillance apps are, ironically, the ones least likely to have the open communication that makes surveillance unnecessary.
And the use of surveillance apps can make that communication harder, not easier. When teenagers know they're being watched, they have less reason to tell their parents where they're going because their parents already know. The conversation that builds trust is replaced by a live map that bypasses it.
What family safety should actually look like
None of this means that technology has no role in family safety. It clearly does. Arrival alerts knowing when someone has reached their destination are genuinely useful. SOS alerts are potentially life-saving.
Real-time updates have their place too, mainly for the moments that actually need them, not as a default setting.
The problem isn't the technology. It's the assumption that more surveillance equals more safety.
Parents seem to agree, in a recent survey 59% said they'd quit an app that treated their family's trust as a data source.
HeyPolo was built on a different assumption: that the goal of family safety is peace of mind, not maximum visibility. That the person sharing their location should be in control of that decision. That safety and trust aren't opposites.

Each family group can set its own norms too, so a teenager's settings don't have to match a parent's. None of this needs to come at the cost of your teen's phone battery either, lighter tracking generally means less drain.
