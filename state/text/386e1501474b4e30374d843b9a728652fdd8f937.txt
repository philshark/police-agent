When people search for what age can you drive, they are usually asking two questions at once, and only one of them has an age as the answer.
A standard learner's permit means supervised practice with a licensed adult in the passenger seat. Solo driving is a separate permission that arrives later, under a different credential, with its own conditions attached.
The scale of that supervised stage is easy to underestimate. A put it plainly: "All but four states require new drivers, under age 18, to hold a learner's permit for at least 6 months." During those months, they noted, "driving is only allowed while being supervised by a licensed adult driver." Six months of somebody sitting next to them is the norm, not a local quirk.
So your family needs to answer three things: which credential your teen holds now, how long they must hold it, and what the state attaches to the next one.
What age can you drive in the United States?
No single national driving age exists, because each state and the District of Columbia writes its own entry ages, holding periods, and restrictions.
Federal law does not set a minimum age for a driver's license the way it sets one for drinking. Your state does, and the next state over will have something different entirely
Most of the country runs some version of Graduated Driver Licensing (GDL), a staged approach that hands new drivers privileges in pieces rather than all at once. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention states that "GDL systems exist in all U.S. states and the District of Columbia (D.C.), but the strength of GDL laws varies by state."
Before you look up an age, decide which permission you mean. Learning under supervision, driving alone with limits, and holding a license with no teen-specific restrictions are three different things, and they rarely arrive in the same year.
Can you drive alone with a permit?
An ordinary learner's permit does not allow solo driving. Every state that issues one attaches a supervising-driver requirement, usually a licensed adult over a set age, sometimes seated in the front passenger seat specifically.
Your teen can practice, log hours, and take the car anywhere the supervising driver agrees to go, but not alone.
The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety describes graduated licensing as three stages. The first is . The second is an intermediate license, granted after a road test, that limits driving in high-risk situations except under supervision. The third is a license with full privileges. Solo driving generally begins at the middle stage, and it generally arrives with conditions still attached.
Some states issue narrow exceptions under names like hardship permit or restricted permit. A state grants one for a documented need, such as getting to work or to medical appointments, with route or time limits written into the credential. Those exist where a legislature created them, and the terms vary by state.
One practical habit saves a lot of confusion: read the credential name printed on the card itself. "Learner's permit," "instruction permit," "junior operator," "provisional," and "intermediate" are not interchangeable, and the name on the card is what your state's licensing authority will check against its own rules. Verify that name on the official state page rather than a summary.
What age can you start driving through each license stage?
The stages run in dependency order, and each one unlocks the next rather than replacing it on a birthday.
Supervised learning comes first. Your teen passes a knowledge test and a vision screening, receives a permit, and drives only with a qualifying adult. States set a minimum holding period for this stage, and many pair it with a required number of logged practice hours and, in some cases, a driver education course.
Restricted solo driving comes next, after the holding period ends and the road test is passed. At this point your teen can drive without an adult, within limits the state sets on night driving, teen passengers, or both.
Full privileges come last, when the teen reaches the state's age threshold or completes the restricted period without the violations the state specifies.
Getting the permit does not start the same clock everywhere. A state that requires 50 logged hours and a completed course can hold a teen at the learner stage well past its stated minimum. The hours take time to accumulate. The CDC describes GDL as a way for new drivers to progressively gain experience and skills under lower-risk conditions, and the requirements are what create the progression.
Can you drive at 15?
At 15, some teens can legally sit behind the wheel with an adult beside them, and some cannot drive on public roads at all. Both are true in the same country, in the same week.
A few states set . The IIHS has reported that Montana allows a learner permit at age 14½ and a license at 15. At the other end sits New Jersey, with a minimum permit age of 16 and a minimum intermediate license age of 17. The IIHS calls that the highest licensing age in the country. A 15-year-old in one state is logging practice hours. A 15-year-old in the other is waiting.
Eligibility for a permit does not authorize solo driving anywhere. A 15-year-old who qualifies for a learner's permit still needs a supervising driver for every trip, and still has the holding period and the road test ahead. The two permissions get collapsed into one question constantly, and the collapse is where families get caught out.
Why states phase in solo driving
Each restriction maps onto a specific risk that shows up in early licensure data.
Inexperience is the first. The CDC reports that "the crash rate per mile driven is about 1.5 times as high for 16-year-old drivers as it is for 18-19-year-old drivers." Holding periods and practice-hour rules move some of that early mileage into the supervised stage.
Darkness is the second. The CDC reports a for teen drivers ages 16-19 about 3 times as high as the rate for adult drivers ages 30-59, per mile driven. Night curfews in the restricted stage are built around that finding.
Passengers are the third area states legislate, and most restricted licenses allowed without an adult in the car.
None of this means a legally licensed 16-year-old is unsafe, and none of it means a birthday makes anyone ready.
Licensing rules reduce how much exposure a new driver has to higher-risk conditions while experience accumulates. Readiness is a separate judgment, and it belongs to you and your teen.

A note for readers outside the United States
Everything above describes U.S. state rules. Minimum ages, credential names, and the point at which solo driving becomes legal differ by country, and often by vehicle type within the same country. In the United Kingdom, GOV.UK says people can at 15 years and nine months. Driving a car on public roads generally begins at 17.
Your national or regional licensing authority is the source that governs your situation.

FAQ
Can you drive at 15?
In some states a 15-year-old can hold a learner's permit and drive with a qualifying supervising adult, and a few states set eligibility even earlier. Other states do not allow any public-road driving at 15. Solo driving at 15 depends on the credential held and the state's rules, and an ordinary learner's permit never allows it.
What age can you start driving?
Supervised driving on a learner's permit typically begins somewhere between 14 and 16 depending on the state. That first eligibility age is only the entry point. Supervising-driver rules, driver education requirements, minimum logged practice hours, and permit holding periods all vary by state and determine how quickly a new driver moves toward solo driving.
What age do you get your driver's license?
The first license permitting solo driving and a full unrestricted license usually arrive at different ages. A restricted, provisional, or intermediate license commonly comes after the learner period and a road test, with night and passenger limits still applied. Full privileges follow later, at an age or after a clean period set by the state.
Can you drive alone with a learner's permit?
Generally no. A standard learner's permit requires a qualified supervising driver, and states specify the supervisor's minimum age and license status. Narrow exceptions exist in some states through hardship or restricted permits issued for a documented need, usually with limits on hours, routes, or destinations written into the credential.
Does a provisional license let you drive alone?
Often yes, within limits. A provisional or intermediate license usually permits unsupervised driving while keeping restrictions on night driving, the number of teen passengers, or in some states specific destinations. The exact conditions come from the issuing state, and they are listed on that state's licensing authority page.
Which state's driving rules apply if you move?
The licensing authority in the state of residence sets the rules for the credential held or applied for there. Transfer procedures for teen drivers differ by state, and some states recalculate holding periods or restrictions when a young driver moves in. Checking with the new state's licensing authority before driving avoids an unpleasant surprise.
Can you drive in another state with a learner's permit?
Recognition of out-of-state learner's permits varies, and supervision rules can differ between the issuing state and the destination state. Some states do not recognize out-of-state permits for young drivers at all. Verifying both sets of rules before the trip is the only reliable way to know whether the drive is legal.
Is there a maximum age for driving?
This article covers minimum ages only. No general maximum driving age applies across U.S. states. Renewal intervals, in-person renewal requirements, vision testing, and medical reporting rules differ by jurisdiction and can change with age, so confirmation with the relevant licensing authority is worthwhile.
