Or there is a group of kids by the lockers who have been at it for three weeks.&nbsp; But these nerves are a bit different.&nbsp; My own version of this was a much smaller thing - merely a gym class I begged out of for a whole term. Deciding the school and its teachers are the enemy is also tempting, but rarely useful. Unless you are looking at gross negligence, treating staff as adversaries makes the plan harder to build and slower to work. Signs that bullying or performance are behind the school anxiety The clearest clue is the calendar.&nbsp; Headaches, stomachaches, nausea, dizziness, and fast shallow breathing that show up on school mornings and vanish by Saturday lunch are telling you something specific. The American Academy of Pediatrics notes on its school avoidance guidance that these symptoms happen most often on school days and are usually absent on weekends. Behavior tracks the same pattern. Watch for a sudden fear of one class or one hallway, skipping lunch, dodging group work, grades sliding, or perfectionism that has turned into hours of rewriting a single paragraph. Tests and presentations get avoided outright. Bullying leaves its own marks. Your child withdraws from friends they used to see every weekend, changes the route they walk, comes home with a cracked phone screen or a missing jacket, or has a scrape they will not explain. StopBullying.gov lists frequent stomachaches, faking illness, declining grades, and not wanting to go to school among the signs adults tend to miss. Anxiety can also make a child look restless, distracted, or flat-out oppositional, which is one reason it gets read as ADHD or as attitude. Patterns show up on paper that you will never spot in the middle of a Tuesday morning. What is happening in your child's body on a school morning Your child's nervous system is doing exactly what it is built to do. Faced with something it reads as a threat, whether that is a group of classmates or a graded presentation, the body pushes out adrenaline and cortisol, the heart rate climbs, breathing goes shallow and fast, and blood moves away from the gut. The stomachache is not invented. It is the digestive system getting deprioritized while the body prepares to run. Which is why "you'll be fine" lands so badly.&nbsp; Your child's body has already decided otherwise, and reassurance aimed at the thinking brain does not reach the part that is currently in charge. Bullying and performance pressure both feed this loop, in slightly different ways. Bullying gives the alarm a genuine target, someone who has actually hurt them, so the response is proportionate. Performance fear attaches to being judged, and the anticipation often runs worse than the event.&nbsp; Avoidance calms the alarm within minutes, which teaches the nervous system that avoiding worked. But the relief is real and short, and the fear comes back bigger the next morning. How to deal with school anxiety at home when bullying or performance is the trigger Start with what you say in the first sixty seconds, because that sets the tone for everything after. Here is a sequence that many families find easier than improvising at 7:40 a.m. Name it without arguing. "Your stomach really hurts. I believe you. Anxiety does that to bodies." Do not debate whether the pain is real. It is. Ask one specific question. "What is the hardest part of today?" You want the actual fear, the third-period presentation or the walk past the lockers, not a general "school." Slow the breathing before you plan anything. Four counts in, six counts out, six or seven rounds, done together. A longer exhale settles the alarm faster than a deep breath in. Write one coping line together. Something they would actually say. "I have gotten through hard mornings before and I can text Dad at lunch." Put it on a card in the front pocket of their bag. Solve the specific problem. If it is the presentation, ask what one change would make it survivable, going first rather than last, or reading from notes. If it is the lockers, plan a different route and a person to walk with. Agree the smallest step that still counts as going. Sometimes that is a full day. Sometimes it is homeroom plus one class, with a pickup time your child knows. Avoid two moves that feel helpful and are not. Blanket reassurance ("nothing bad will happen")&nbsp; gives your child nothing to hold when something does. Pressure to toughen up adds shame to fear, and a child carrying both tells you less next time. Build the ladder on paper. Pick the specific fear, then break the distance into steps your child helps rank: walk past the building on a Sunday, go in for fifteen minutes with you in the parking lot, stay for one class they like, add a harder class, then a full day. Attendance is the goal, and each rung stays where it is until it stops being frightening. The foundations underneath all of this are boring and they work. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that children aged 6 to 12 sleep 9 to 12 hours and teenagers aged 13 to 18 sleep 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours, and links shorter sleep to problems with attention, behavior, learning, and depression.&nbsp; Movement helps too.&nbsp; The CDC recommends 60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily for ages 6 to 17, with brain-health benefits including better academic performance and fewer symptoms of depression. Give mornings fifteen minutes more slack than they strictly need, so a wobble does not turn into a missed bus. Rehearse the social parts out loud, badly, in the kitchen. What your child says if someone is unkind, kept short and unrewarding: "Not interested." How they ask a teacher for help without an audience, which usually means catching them at the end of class rather than raising a hand.&nbsp; And agree in advance how they can reach you during the day, one text at a set time, so it feels like backup and not a check on whether they cracked. When your head says "everyone will laugh," try "some people might not care, and I have gotten through this before." You are aiming for realistic, not cheerful. Ask one adult, in specific terms. "There is a problem with some people in fourth period and I need help with it" works better than "I am fine." Pick the adult you actually like. A teacher, a counselor, a coach. Set up one check-in. Ask a parent to text you the same thing at the same time each day, or agree that you will text them after lunch. Knowing it is coming helps more than you would think. Plans made together hold up better than plans made in secret. Working with the school on bullying, workload, and accommodations Go in assuming the staff want this fixed. Most of them do, and arriving convinced otherwise costs you the working relationship you are about to need. Report bullying in a specific order. Start with the classroom teacher if the incidents are in one room, and go to the counselor or an administrator if they span the day or involve safety. Put it in writing, even after a phone call, so there is a record with a date on it. Document as you go: date, time, location, who was involved, who witnessed it, what was said or done, and what the school did next. Screenshots for anything online. Vague reports get vague responses. A list with times and places gets a plan. Then follow up. Ask what will change, who is responsible for it, and when you will hear back. Put that date in your calendar and use it. On formal support, the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights explains in a 2024 fact sheet that under Section 504, a student with an anxiety disorder can be a student with a disability if the anxiety substantially limits one or more major life activities, and that reasonable modifications must be individualized to the student.&nbsp; Learning and concentrating both count as major life activities. In practice, accommodations might look like extra time on tests, presenting to the teacher alone rather than the class, a pass to leave the room when panic rises, a named staff member as a first point of contact, or a modified workload during a hard stretch. When to bring in professional help and what treatment involves Bring in a professional when school anxiety keeps your child out of school or out of class, when it has run for weeks rather than days, or when you see panic attacks, big changes in sleep or appetite, or any talk of self-harm.&nbsp; That last one goes straight to the crisis steps at the end of this article. Anxiety at this level is common. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for anxiety in children and adolescents aged 8 to 18, and reports that 7.8% of children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 had a current anxiety disorder in the 2018-2019 National Survey of Children's Health. Cognitive behavioral therapy with planned exposure is the first-line treatment, and the evidence behind it is strong. A 2020 Cochrane review found that compared with waitlist or no treatment, CBT increased post-treatment remission of primary anxiety diagnoses from 17.8% to 49.4% across 39 studies. What that looks like in a room, week by week, is more ordinary than the acronym suggests.&nbsp; Early sessions are collaborative goal-setting, where the therapist asks your child what they want to be able to do again, and the answer is usually something concrete like eating lunch in the cafeteria.&nbsp; Then comes learning to catch the anxious thought and check it against evidence. Then coping skills, breathing and muscle relaxation, practiced when calm so they are available when not.&nbsp; Then the exposure work, built as a ladder tied to your child's actual fears: watching a video of a presentation, presenting to one sibling, presenting to the therapist, presenting to two classmates after school, presenting to the class. Homework between sessions is where most of the change happens, and progress is measured in rungs climbed. A note on mindfulness, since schools offer it often. A systematic review of school-based mindfulness interventions found significant improvement in stress but no significant effect on depression and anxiety compared with active control groups.&nbsp; Useful alongside real treatment. Not the plan when your child is missing school. That same reassurance matters after the bell too, during the stretch when a&nbsp;latchkey kid is home without you and working through a different kind of quiet. After a bad incident with a classmate, or on the morning of an exam, that knowledge does a lot of quiet work. Keep it relational and predictable. A text at the same time each day that says nothing more than "thinking of you, see you at 3:30." An agreed check-in after lunch or after the class that scares them. Ten minutes on Sunday evening looking at the week ahead together, so Monday morning has no surprises. FAQ How do I tell if my kid's school anxiety is from bullying or from academic pressure? The two can look nearly identical from the outside: stomachaches before school, sudden reluctance to go, a drop in grades. Performance anxiety tends to spike around tests, projects, or report cards. Bullying-related anxiety tends to spike around specific times or places, like lunch, the bus, or a particular class. If your kid can't point to a moment that sets it off, that's worth paying attention to on its own. How do I talk to my kid about school anxiety without making them shut down? Ask about the day before you ask about the anxiety. "What happened at lunch" gets further than "are you being bullied." Kids who are struggling, whether it's a bully or a grade, tend to answer specific questions more easily than broad ones. And they answer better when it doesn't feel like an interrogation they have to survive. Should I contact the school if I think performance pressure is coming from a teacher? Yes, and sooner than most parents do. A conversation with the teacher, or the school counselor if that feels safer, usually clarifies whether the pressure is about your kid's actual performance or about how it's being communicated. Teachers aren't always aware of how a comment lands on a kid who's already anxious. How can I stay aware without hovering over every part of my kid's school day? You don't need to know every detail of the school day to know your kid is okay. Knowing they made it to school, made it home, and are where they said they'd be covers most of the actual worry, without turning into a running interrogation about who said what at lunch. That's a narrower kind of reassurance than most parents start with, and it's usually enough. Is school anxiety the same as school refusal? School anxiety describes distress about going to or being at school. School refusal describes what happens when that distress becomes intense enough to keep a child from attending part or all of the day. Some families and clinicians prefer the term emotionally based school avoidance, since school refusal implies a deliberate choice. Should a child be kept home when they say they feel too sick to go? Once a pediatrician has ruled out medical causes, keeping a child home tends to relieve the anxiety in the short term and strengthen the avoidance pattern over time. Many clinicians recommend keeping some school contact most days, even a partial day, while a graded return plan is built with the school. How long should parents wait before contacting a doctor? Cleveland Clinic advises that if school refusal, significant anxiety, or physical symptoms continue for two weeks or longer, parents should talk with a children's healthcare provider. Can a child with anxiety get formal accommodations at school? Under Section 504, a student with an anxiety disorder can be a student with a disability when the anxiety substantially limits one or more major life activities such as learning or concentrating, and any reasonable modifications must be individualized to that student. Does location sharing make an anxious child more anxious? It depends entirely on who controls it. Sharing that a child sets up themselves, for a defined window such as the walk to school, generally works as reassurance. Continuous monitoring the child did not agree to tends to read as distrust and can add to the pressure they are already under. &nbsp;

Keep bullying on its own track. If your child is being actively harmed and the school has not moved, it is reasonable to push for stronger safety measures, in writing, alongside the attendance plan.

When to bring in professional help and what treatment involves
Bring in a professional when school anxiety keeps your child out of school or out of class, when it has run for weeks rather than days, or when you see panic attacks, big changes in sleep or appetite, or any talk of self-harm.
That last one goes straight to the crisis steps at the end of this article.
Anxiety at this level is common. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for anxiety in children and adolescents aged 8 to 18, and reports that 7.8% of children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 had a current anxiety disorder in the 2018-2019 National Survey of Children's Health.
Cognitive behavioral therapy with planned exposure is the first-line treatment, and the evidence behind it is strong. A 2020 Cochrane review found that compared with waitlist or no treatment, CBT increased post-treatment remission of primary anxiety diagnoses from 17.8% to 49.4% across 39 studies.
What that looks like in a room, week by week, is more ordinary than the acronym suggests.
Early sessions are collaborative goal-setting, where the therapist asks your child what they want to be able to do again, and the answer is usually something concrete like eating lunch in the cafeteria.
Then comes learning to catch the anxious thought and check it against evidence. Then coping skills, breathing and muscle relaxation, practiced when calm so they are available when not.
Then the exposure work, built as a ladder tied to your child's actual fears: watching a video of a presentation, presenting to one sibling, presenting to the therapist, presenting to two classmates after school, presenting to the class. Homework between sessions is where most of the change happens, and progress is measured in rungs climbed.
A note on mindfulness, since schools offer it often. A systematic review of school-based mindfulness interventions found significant improvement in stress but no significant effect on depression and anxiety compared with active control groups.
Useful alongside real treatment. Not the plan when your child is missing school.
Using check-ins and tools in a way that feels like care, not surveillance
Anxious children hold up better when they know two things: they can reach you, and you can reach them.
That same reassurance matters after the bell too, during the stretch when a latchkey kid is home without you and working through a different kind of quiet. After a bad incident with a classmate, or on the morning of an exam, that knowledge does a lot of quiet work.
Keep it relational and predictable. A text at the same time each day that says nothing more than "thinking of you, see you at 3:30." An agreed check-in after lunch or after the class that scares them.
Ten minutes on Sunday evening looking at the week ahead together, so Monday morning has no surprises. Building that into the school schedule means it happens without anyone remembering to suggest it.
Location sharing can sit inside that arrangement, as long as your child sets the terms. A shared group for the school run or an after-school activity, where they decide when it is on and how precise it is, tells you they arrived. Watching their route all day tells you something else entirely, and teenagers can spot the difference instantly.

If bullying or anxiety escalates to a crisis
Thoughts of self-harm, talk of wanting to die, or behavior that makes you fear for your child's immediate safety are medical emergencies. The trigger at school does not change that.
Call or text 988 to reach the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, or use the chat at 988lifeline.org.
The Lifeline is available 24/7/365, free and confidential, for people experiencing mental health struggles or emotional distress.
In an immediate, life-threatening emergency, call 911.
Where you can, remove or secure medications, firearms, and other lethal means at home. Tell the crisis counselor and any clinician you see about the bullying or the performance pressure, because that context shapes what follow-up care they recommend.
Closing
Your child's body is not lying to them, and the fear is not a bid for a day off. Treat the alarm as real, take the specific threat seriously with the school, and rebuild attendance in steps small enough that each one succeeds. That is the work, and it is slower than anyone wants it to be.

FAQ
How do I tell if my kid's school anxiety is from bullying or from academic pressure?
The two can look nearly identical from the outside: stomachaches before school, sudden reluctance to go, a drop in grades. Performance anxiety tends to spike around tests, projects, or report cards. Bullying-related anxiety tends to spike around specific times or places, like lunch, the bus, or a particular class. If your kid can't point to a moment that sets it off, that's worth paying attention to on its own.
How do I talk to my kid about school anxiety without making them shut down?
Ask about the day before you ask about the anxiety. "What happened at lunch" gets further than "are you being bullied." Kids who are struggling, whether it's a bully or a grade, tend to answer specific questions more easily than broad ones. And they answer better when it doesn't feel like an interrogation they have to survive.
Should I contact the school if I think performance pressure is coming from a teacher?
Yes, and sooner than most parents do. A conversation with the teacher, or the school counselor if that feels safer, usually clarifies whether the pressure is about your kid's actual performance or about how it's being communicated. Teachers aren't always aware of how a comment lands on a kid who's already anxious.
How can I stay aware without hovering over every part of my kid's school day?
You don't need to know every detail of the school day to know your kid is okay. Knowing they made it to school, made it home, and are where they said they'd be covers most of the actual worry, without turning into a running interrogation about who said what at lunch. That's a narrower kind of reassurance than most parents start with, and it's usually enough.
Is school anxiety the same as school refusal?
School anxiety describes distress about going to or being at school. School refusal describes what happens when that distress becomes intense enough to keep a child from attending part or all of the day. Some families and clinicians prefer the term emotionally based school avoidance, since school refusal implies a deliberate choice.
Should a child be kept home when they say they feel too sick to go?
Once a pediatrician has ruled out medical causes, keeping a child home tends to relieve the anxiety in the short term and strengthen the avoidance pattern over time. Many clinicians recommend keeping some school contact most days, even a partial day, while a graded return plan is built with the school.
How long should parents wait before contacting a doctor?
Cleveland Clinic advises that if school refusal, significant anxiety, or physical symptoms continue for two weeks or longer, parents should talk with a children's healthcare provider.
Can a child with anxiety get formal accommodations at school?
Under Section 504, a student with an anxiety disorder can be a student with a disability when the anxiety substantially limits one or more major life activities such as learning or concentrating, and any reasonable modifications must be individualized to that student.
Does location sharing make an anxious child more anxious?
It depends entirely on who controls it. Sharing that a child sets up themselves, for a defined window such as the walk to school, generally works as reassurance. Continuous monitoring the child did not agree to tends to read as distrust and can add to the pressure they are already under.
