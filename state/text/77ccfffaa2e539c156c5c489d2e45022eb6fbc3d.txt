How to Prepare Kids for School Before the First Day
Your child doesn't need to read a word before day one. They need familiar habits, a rehearsed goodbye, and a lunch container they can open at 12:15 without help.
Phil
8 min read•August 10, 2026
The clothes are on the chair.
The water bottle is standing by the bag near the door, filled and already sweating a little.
Then comes the question, asked in a small voice: will you be there to pick me up?
I remember both asking the question and being asked it - and not having a good answer.
When you look up how to prepare kids for school, most of what you find reads like a test to pass.
But in my experience, rehearsing the shape of the day works better. Your child gets to practice the parts they'll actually face alone, and you stop measuring them against a standard nobody agrees on.
What readiness for school genuinely looks like
Readiness covers physical development, thinking and language, adaptive skills like dressing and eating, and how a child manages their own feelings and other people.
That's a lot for a human that's only just stopped eating sand - or may still be.
What's more, those areas don't move at the same speed inside every child. A four-year-old can hold a marker beautifully and still cry when a routine changes, and neither fact predicts the other.
Fayette County Public Schools puts it plainly in its readiness guidance, noting that children grow and develop at different rates and that skills vary from child to child.
A checklist read that way becomes a map of what to practice this month, and if you're getting more than one child ready at once, a checklist for two kids keeps the two lists from turning into two separate projects. Read the other way, as a grading sheet, it mostly just makes the last two weeks before school worse.
The framing I've come around to is what the UK's Best Start in Life guidance describes: confidence, familiar habits, a sense of belonging, and being able to join in.
None of those require a child to read a word.
A countdown you can follow to the first day
Preparation is better when it's organized by time rather than by good intentions. Each stage below rehearses something real.
Several weeks before
Bedtime tends to drift over a long break, and pulling it back in one night rarely works. Sleep is the first thing you place when you make a school schedule, and everything else fits around it.
Prevent Child Abuse America suggests easing children into the school sleep schedule several weeks in advance rather than the night before. Moving bedtime and wake time in small steps gives everyone room to adjust.
You might also use these weeks to learn the school's arrival and collection procedures, along with the rest of the back to school safety ground: the bus stop, the drop-off line, and who is allowed to collect your child.
The final week
Some families find that visiting the setting once, even just the gate and the yard, does more than a week of talking about it.
If a visit isn't possible, walking to school or driving the route counts.
Lunch containers deserve a dry run at the kitchen table, because a child who can't open their yogurt at 12:15 has won't ask for help. Choosing a bag or a lunch box together gives your child a small piece of the decision.
The night before
Pack the bag, sort the food, and put the clothes somewhere visible. Then keep the evening ordinary.
A big send-off dinner raises the temperature of a day that's already carrying enough.
The morning itself
Give yourselves a little extra time in the first morning for the shoe that'll inevitably go missing.
A familiar breakfast asks nothing new of a child who's about to meet twenty new faces. Begin Learning recommends a visual morning checklist, which some children follow more easily than a spoken list of five things. Then the goodbye you agreed on, in the words you agreed on.

Handling the nerves that come with kids going to school for the first time
Broad reassurance if a kid is nervous tends to bounce off a small human who's struggling to explain what's happening. Saying "you look worried" gives a child something accurate to agree with, and often opens the door to the specific thing they've been chewing on, which is usually smaller and more practical than you expect. New school year nerves work the same way further up the school, where the specific thing is usually a corridor or a lunch table.

Where will I put my coat. Who takes me to the bathroom.
Playing school at home, where your child gets to be the teacher and you find out what they think happens between drop-off and lunch gives you an insight into what they're anticipating.
Practice short goodbyes and settling on a goodbye routine ahead of time, which is the part I found very effective.
A predictable ending to drop-off, the same two lines and the same wave every morning, gives a child something to hold onto. Tears may still come. A rehearsed goodbye doesn't prevent them, it just means nobody is improvising.
Rehearsing the parts of the day you can actually visit
Where a school allows it, seeing the grounds, the entrance your child will use, and the classroom door removes a whole category of unknowns.
If possible, try attending pre-year events and finding the classroom or locker in advance.
Practicing the journey matters as much as the destination. Walk it, ride it, or drive it once, and say out loud who will be waiting at the end of the day and where they'll stand.
Once the clocks change, that same journey is worth a second look for darker evenings walking home, since the route that looked fine in September can look different by November.
A child who can picture the collection point has one less thing to hold in their head at 3 pm.
A first-time starter benefits from the concrete version, hands on the gate, feet on the route. A returning child usually needs less of that and more about what changed, a new teacher or a moved classroom.
A child changing schools carries the biggest load, and keeping hobbies and home routines steady through the move gives them something that hasn't changed.

The small skills your child will use between the bells
Most of the school day runs on tasks nobody puts on a readiness list.
Asking a teacher for help.
Saying they're thirsty.
Getting a coat on before everyone else has gone outside.
These are practice areas, not entry requirements, and a child who can do four and not the other two is doing fine.
A child might ask for water through speech, through signing, through picture cards, or through a communication device.
What you're rehearsing is the request itself, using whichever method your child already relies on, so the method holds up in a room full of strangers.
Worth practicing at home when the moment arises rather than as a drill:
Asking an adult for help, in your child's own communication method
Saying or indicating they need the bathroom, and washing hands afterward
Getting a coat, shoes, and fastenings on and off
Opening lunch containers, wrappers, and bottles without help
Keeping track of a bag and a water bottle across a room
Taking turns and waiting a short time for something they want
Games that build the skills classrooms ask for
The National Association for the Education of Young Children recommends playful cue-and-response activities for practicing attention, following directions, and flexible thinking.
Red Light, Green Light does this well.
The caller stands at one end of an open space and shouts green light, and the children move toward them. The caller then yells red light, which stops everyone where they are. Anyone caught moving after the red light call and beofre the green light call has to go back and start again.
Now try breaking the rules on purpose. Swap the meanings halfway through, so green means stop. Children have to hold the new rule and override the old one, which is the same effort they'll spend when a teacher changes the plan mid-morning.
Four more that cost nothing:
Ask open questions about the day, the kind that can't be answered with yes
Talk about words connected to whatever your child is obsessed with this month - dinosaurs or soap or cows
Count things during ordinary chores, forks on the table, socks out of the machine
Stop mid-page while reading together and ask what they think happens next
All of this belongs in daily life rather than in a lesson at the kitchen table three days before term.
Turning the last week into academic practice tends to raise the stakes for a child who was already unsure.
Coordinating with the school around SEND and communication
Where your child has special educational needs or a disability, the preparation works better when the school knows the plan before day one rather than discovering it at 9:30 am.
Things worth putting in writing to the school ahead of time:
Your child's established communication method
Sensory preferences and what they find difficult
Any toileting support
Calming strategies that already work at home.
Asking the school what accommodations or extra orientation visits they can offer often turns up options nobody mentioned in the welcome pack.
When the first day goes badly
Some children cry.
Some refuse to let go of a sleeve.
The agreed goodbye still applies, kept short and kept the same, because a slipping-out-the-side-door exit teaches a child to watch you instead of settling in.
Asking the school later how your child settled after you left gives you information you can't get from the doorway, and staff usually have a clear answer.
Where distress keeps returning, or where it starts affecting sleep, eating, physical comfort, or ordinary daily functioning, a conversation with your child's teacher and an appropriate health professional will get you guidance built around your child.
That's outside what any article can offer.
Deciding your own part in the goodbye
Preparation includes you.
Settling the exact wording of the goodbye, who is collecting your child, and how you contact the school if you need to, all before you arrive at the gate, means you're not making decisions in front of a child who is reading your face for information.
You're allowed to find it hard.
Hiding every feeling isn't the goal, and children spot the performance anyway. What helps is keeping the plan steady while you feel it.
Your child will meet the first day with whatever they've practiced, which is more than they had a month ago and less than a checklist would demand.
That's the honest position.
What you can hold onto is that they know how to ask for help, they know who's collecting them, and you know who to call if something isn't right.
For the older child making that journey alone, the live question is when kids go out alone rather than whether they can manage the classroom.

FAQ
How early should I start preparing my child for school?
Several weeks ahead is a reasonable starting point where the timing allows, particularly for sleep. Prevent Child Abuse America recommends easing children into the school sleep schedule several weeks in advance. Families with only a few days left can still get value from route familiarity, practicing self-care tasks, and settling on the goodbye plan.
What should kids going to school for the first time be able to do?
No single checklist fits every child, because children develop at different rates. The most useful areas to practice are communicating needs through whatever method the child already uses, following familiar directions, managing basic self-care with the support that's available, and knowing which adult to approach for help.
How can I help my child with first-day nerves?
Naming the feeling out loud, rather than reassuring broadly, tends to open the conversation. Answering concrete questions about coats, bathrooms, and lunch removes specific unknowns. Reading a story about starting school, visiting the setting where possible, and rehearsing a short goodbye all give a child something familiar to hold onto.
What should I do if my child cries at drop-off?
Following the agreed goodbye and leaving as planned gives the child a predictable ending rather than an uncertain one. Asking the school later how the child settled usually provides reassurance the doorway can't. Distress that keeps returning or affects daily functioning warrants a conversation with the teacher and an appropriate health professional.
How can I prepare a child with SEND for school?
Contacting the school early to share the child's communication method, whether speech, signing, picture cards, or a device, gives staff time to prepare. Sensory preferences, toileting support, transition cues, and established calming strategies belong in that conversation too, along with questions about available accommodations.
Should I use location sharing for the school run?
For a phone-age child traveling with more independence, the conversation comes before the app. Agreeing who sees the location, how precise it is, and when sharing stops keeps the arrangement mutual. Time-limited sharing or an arrival alert can support a check-in plan both people signed up to.
