Teen Drivers Crash More Than Any Other Age Group. What Parents Can Do
The first months behind the wheel are the riskiest. Here's what the research says, and the simple steps that help families feel more at ease.
By the HeyPolo Team · November 2026 · 5 min read
It's 10:40 on a Friday night. Your daughter texted that she was leaving her friend's house twenty minutes ago. It's a fifteen-minute drive.
You check your phone. Nothing. You tell yourself she probably stopped for gas, or forgot to text. You check again.
Almost every parent of a new driver knows this feeling. And it's not just nerves: the numbers show that the teen years really are the riskiest time to be on the road.

What the numbers say
According to the , the risk of a car crash is higher for teens ages 16 to 19 than for any other age group. Motor vehicle crashes are the leading cause of death for US teens.
A few findings stand out for parents:
Almost three times the fatal crash rate. Per mile driven, teen drivers have a fatal crash rate almost three times as high as drivers 20 and older.
The first months are the riskiest. Crash risk is particularly high right after teens get their license. Per mile, 16-year-olds crash about 1.5 times as often as 18- and 19-year-olds.
Nights and weekends. At night, the fatal crash rate for teen drivers is about three times that of adults ages 30 to 59. In 2020, 44% of teen crash deaths happened between 9 p.m. and 6 a.m., and half happened on a Friday, Saturday or Sunday.
Passengers raise the risk. Each additional teen or young adult passenger increases the crash risk for an unsupervised teen driver.
The CDC points to a few reasons: inexperience, speeding, distraction and not wearing a seat belt. In 2019, 39% of high school students who drove said they had texted or emailed while driving in the past month.
In other words, the moments when teens are most at risk are often the moments when parents aren't there: late at night, on weekends, with friends in the car.
The minutes after a crash
Most parents assume that if something happened, they'd hear right away. Their teen would call. Or a friend would. Or someone at the scene.
Often that's true. But after a serious crash, the driver may be hurt, in shock, or unable to reach their phone. Friends in the car may be just as shaken. And late at night on a quiet road, there may be no one else around.
That's the gap many families don't think about until they need to: the time between a crash and the moment someone they love knows about it.
Phones can now help close it. Modern smartphones have motion sensors that can recognize the sudden forces of a serious crash. Some newer phones have crash detection built in, depending on the model. Family safety apps can add another layer: alerting the people who would want to know first, with the location of where it happened.
What parents can do
The good news: the says teen crashes are preventable, and parents play an important role. Here are five steps that help.
Keep practicing together. Driving is a skill that takes practice. The CDC recommends supervised driving in different conditions: at night, in the rain, on highways.
Set clear rules for the riskiest times. Agree on limits for night driving and for friends in the car, especially in the first months. The CDC offers a free to make it official.
Seat belt, every trip. At least half of teen drivers and passengers ages 16 to 19 who died in crashes in 2020 weren't wearing one. Seat belts cut serious injuries and deaths by about half.
Phone away while driving. Distraction is especially dangerous for new drivers. A simple rule, like phone in the glovebox, removes the temptation.
Stay involved, and set up a safety net. The CDC notes that parental involvement can reduce risky driving, and that tools giving feedback to both teens and parents have helped. Crash alerts add one more layer: if something happens and your teen can't call, you still find out.
