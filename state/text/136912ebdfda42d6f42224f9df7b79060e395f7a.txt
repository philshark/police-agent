Your Teen Is Learning to Drive From Watching You
My daughter started calling out the yellow lights before her feet could reach the pedals. Years later I was the one in the passenger seat, on the way home from her aunt's house, and I watched her speed up for a yellow on the same stretch of road. I ever so slightly as she sped up a little farther away from the lights than I would have, but then it dawned on me - I knew exactly where she'd learned it. Your teen is learning to drive from watching you, and those lessons started long before they had a permit. Catherine McDonald of the Center for Injury Research and Prevention at Children's Hospital of Philadelphia put it plainly in 2020. "By the time a teenager gets a learner's permit,they probably have been watching their parents drive for 13 to 14 years." None of it was planned. It happened on school runs and grocery trips, stuck in traffic and running late. Most of us speed up for a yellow now and then, or take a call we shouldn't. That doesn't make you a bad driver or a bad parent. It does mean the most protective thing you can do before you hand over the keys is take an honest look at your own habits, and keep looking once the license arrives. Your teen is learning to drive from watching you long before the permit New drivers don't start from scratch. They start with ten years of watching you, and every new road gets measured against that. Then formal practice starts, and you're in the passenger seat. A Safe Kids Worldwide study in 2016 found that 84% of teens and 88% of parents said practice with parents made up the biggest part of learning to drive, and 75% of teens called it the most helpful part. So you're the example and the instructor, which is a lot of responsibility for someone who's also just trying to get to work on time. Researchers put data recorders in the cars of 42 families in Virginia, so teens and their parents were driving the same cars on the same roads. Over the first 18 months with a license, the&nbsp;newly licensed teens in that study had a crash and near-crash rate 3.91 times higher than their parents. In other words, it's the same car on the same streets, with nearly four times the close calls. For every close call you have, they're having roughly four, and you'll hear about almost none of them, because a near miss doesn't leave a dent. It was a small study, but the recorders captured what each driver actually did, so nobody's memory came into it. Your driving is the standard they're working toward, and whatever you've made normal is where they'll aim. Teens notice more of your driving than you think To you, a glance at your phone at a red light is two seconds on a map. From the passenger seat, it looks like what drivers do at red lights. That picture starts forming years before they can drive. If you have a younger one at home, the rules you set with your kid&rsquo;s first phone are the first draft of the ones they&rsquo;ll bring into the car. Researchers surveyed 403 parent and teen pairs about distracted driving. How often a teen believed their parent drove distracted predicted the teen's own distracted driving about twice as strongly as what the parent reported, with a correlation of .41 against .20 (Carter et al., 2014, Journal of Adolescent Health). In other words, what your kid thinks you do matters more than what you think you do. Their version is the one they copy. Their version is harsher, too. On a five-point scale, teens rated their parents' distraction at 1.99, while parents gave themselves 1.59. The phone check you'd call rare, your teen would call regular. The&nbsp;same survey of parent and teen pairs found 92% of the teens regularly did at least one distracting thing behind the wheel, counting eating and changing the music as well as using a phone. The data is from 2012 and the sample skewed white and higher-income, so read the exact numbers as a direction. It's a clear one. A National Safety Council survey in 2015 found that 91% of parents who use their phone while driving do it with their teen in the car, and 88% see themselves as one of their teen's main driving teachers. Put those together and almost every parent who checks a phone behind the wheel is doing it in front of the person they're teaching. Deborah Hersman, who led the Council at the time, said in 2015 that "'Do as I say, not as I do' can be fatal." It's harsh, and it's hard to argue with. So skip the lecture. Your teen already knows what you do, and lectures have a poor track record with this age group anyway, which is a big part of why scary crash stories don&rsquo;t slow a teen down. Talk about the speed and habits you can both see, so it never turns into your word against theirs.
