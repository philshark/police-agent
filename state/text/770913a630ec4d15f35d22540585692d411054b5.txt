The Guilt of Living Far From Your Parents
Millions of us are doing it. Nobody talks about what it actually feels like.
Sinead
2 min read•June 10, 2026
You moved away for all the right reasons. Work. A partner. A city that felt like yours. A life you were building.
Of the 34 million Americans who care for older family members, roughly 15 percent are long-distance caregivers , living at least an hour's drive from the older adults they're helping to look after. Long-distance caregiving can cost an average of nearly $12,000 each year in care-related expenses.
But the financial cost is only part of it. The emotional cost is harder to measure and harder to talk about.
There's the guilt , persistent, low-level, and almost completely immune to logic. You know you can't uproot your life. You know your parents wouldn't want you to. None of that stops the guilt.

The particular anxiety of not knowing
The hardest part of living far from ageing parents isn't the distance itself. It's the not knowing.
When they're right there with you, you can see. You can notice that they're moving a little more slowly, or that the kitchen is less tidy than usual, or that they're repeating themselves in a way they didn't used to. Distance takes that away.
According to a 2021 study of adult-child caregivers, 13% live between 21 and 59 minutes away from their elderly parent, while a further 13% live an hour or more away. That's a significant portion of families living with this particular quiet anxiety , the kind that doesn't have a clean resolution and doesn't go away.
The Sunday night phone call
Most adult children with ageing parents have a version of this ritual: a regular call, usually a weekend, that functions as much as a welfare check as a conversation. A weekly call can't tell you whether they've been eating properly, or whether they got to their appointment, or whether the dizziness came back.
What actually helps
The thing most adult children actually want is simple: to know that their parents are moving through their day safely. To get a notification if something feels wrong.
HeyPolo was built for exactly this , not as a surveillance tool, but as a way to stay gently connected across distance. An elderly parent who uses it stays in control of their own information. They choose when they share their location and with whom. They can set arrival alerts so their family gets a quiet notification when they reach their doctor's appoint, or get home from a walk, without anyone having to ask.
If you ever want more context, you can also glance back at their recent location history instead of asking them to recount their day.
If something does go wrong , if there's a fall, a health scare, a moment of genuine need , the SOS alert sends an instant notification to everyone in the family group. Not the ambulance, not a stranger, but the people who matter most. This matters even more on older phones, worth checking how location sharing affects battery life before you set it up.
It's a similar instinct to the one many parents feel about teenage independence, just pointed in the other direction.
Your parents raised you to build your own life. You're allowed to live it. You just need to know they're okay while you do.
