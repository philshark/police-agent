How Does Location Sharing Affect Your Battery?
Yes, location sharing drains your battery, and how much depends mostly on whether your phone is moving.
We can a test to figure out exactly how much it drains.
Phil
7 min read•September 15, 2026
HeyPolo and Battery Life
Most location-sharing apps are built for constant, always-on tracking  which means they're also constantly working in the background, using GPS, sending data, and running processes your battery has to pay for.
HeyPolo uses more battery while you're moving and very little while you're still.
The app checks your location when your phone starts moving and stops using GPS once you've stopped, since there's no reason to keep recalculating the position of a phone sitting on a desk.
Battery optimisation is something the HeyPolo team actively works on. The app is continuously updated with performance improvements, so the experience gets better over time rather than staying static. Our test results are further down, with charts for a phone left still for a week, a 600 km road trip and a week of commuting.
Apple's Find My uses less battery than HeyPolo or Life360, because it only asks for your location when someone opens the app to look for you.
The trade-off is that Find My can show where you are now or where you were last seen, with no record of your trips. HeyPolo keeps location updating in the background so your group can see those trips, and that background work is where the extra battery goes.
"Find My checks your location only on demand, so for the battery it's the lowest impact. But you can't check where you were yesterday. You only get the current or last known location."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
How Location Sharing Apps Affect Battery Life
Five things decide how much battery a location-sharing app uses:
Whether you're moving or still
How and if it runs in the background
How it finds your location
How often it updates
How strong your signal is.
"When you're not moving, the impact is kind of zero. Maybe 2% per day, maybe rarely more than 1%."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
Movement matters most for apps like HeyPolo and Life360. These apps switch GPS on when your phone starts moving and off when it stops, so a day at home costs very little battery and a long drive costs a lot. In our stationary test, the HeyPolo phone used less than 1 percentage point more battery per day than the phone without it.
Background activity is where most of the drain happens. When a location-sharing app runs in the background, it keeps your phone's processor and radio hardware active, and every location it sends uses mobile data, which costs battery too. The more often the app checks and reports your location, the more battery it uses.
GPS vs. network-based location makes a significant difference. GPS is the most accurate method but also the most battery-intensive, because it requires your device to communicate directly with satellites. Many apps use a combination of GPS, Wi-Fi positioning, and mobile network data to estimate location. This hybrid approach tends to be more battery-friendly than GPS alone without sacrificing much accuracy.
If you're picking an app for a mixed iPhone-and-Android household specifically, our guide on how to covers the options side by side.
Update frequency is directly tied to drain. An app that pings your location every few seconds will consume considerably more battery than one that updates every few minutes. This is one of the most important things to check when choosing a location-sharing app, and one of the least visible to the average user. HeyPolo's updates while you're on the move and pauses GPS once you've stopped.
Weak signal drains battery faster. A study in IEEE Access found GPS apps used 13% of battery on average in areas with good GPS signal, and where GPS signal was weak. The impact varies widely by device, settings, and how often the app updates your location.
Weak mobile signal has a similar effect. In a city, your phone hops between nearby cell towers with almost no effort. With poor coverage, it keeps searching for a better tower, and it's believed that some phones also boost their antenna to catch weaker signals, which uses more power. This happens with or without a location app installed, but it adds up on a long drive through the countryside.
"When the signal is weak, the phone is always trying to find a better signal."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
Running two location apps at once, such as HeyPolo and Google Maps, uses more battery, though probably not double. Your phone limits how often apps can get a location and can pass one GPS reading to both apps. Each app still sends its own data, and that costs battery. We haven't measured this yet, and Algirdas plans to.
"I'm very certain it has an impact, maybe not double, because the phone is still throttling how often you get the location."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
How to Reduce Battery Drain While Using Location Sharing Apps
Choosing "While Using the App" saves battery, but location-sharing apps like HeyPolo then stop updating in the background. With Always Allow, the app can reach your phone's GPS whenever it needs to, which is how it records trips and keeps your group up to date.
With While Using the App, your phone blocks GPS for background processes, so the app only gets your location while it's open on your screen.
HeyPolo only uses the permission you choose to give it, and it doesn't try to work around a While Using the App setting.
"You won't have any trips, you won't have anything with this permission, because it's just impossible. We respect all the permissions and only use location when a user intentionally gives us permission."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
iPhone vs. Android: Background Location Differences
iPhone and Android both handle background location well, but iOS is stricter. Apple limits how long and how heavily apps can run in the background, and shuts down any app that pushes too hard. HeyPolo is built to collect a location and send it quickly, without heavy processing on your phone, so it stays within those limits.
iOS also gives apps less freedom to detect when you start moving. One tool Apple offers is the significant location change trigger, which wakes an app when your phone connects to a different cell tower, without using GPS. Android gives developers more flexibility, though most things work the same on both platforms.
"Apple doesn't let you do heavy things in the background. It will just forcibly kill your process, and you get nothing."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
Android
Enable Battery Saver mode when your battery drops low. This typically reduces location update frequency automatically.
Check Settings → Battery → Battery usage to see which apps are consuming the most. If a location app is near the top, review its update frequency settings.
Turn off Bluetooth scanning and Wi-Fi scanning under Location settings if you don't need them. These run in the background to assist location accuracy but add to overall drain.
Use Adaptive Battery (available on Android 9 and above), which learns your usage patterns and restricts background activity for apps you use infrequently.
iPhone
Under Settings → Battery, enable Low Power Mode when charge is running low. This reduces background refresh, including location updates, across all apps.
Check Settings → Battery → Battery usage by app to identify which apps are consuming the most. A high percentage from a location app is a signal to review its settings.
Disable Precise Location for apps that don't need your exact coordinates  available per app under Location Services, and uses less battery than full GPS precision.
For HeyPolo, leaving Precise Location on keeps your group's map accurate.
Turn off Background App Refresh for location apps under Settings → General → Background App Refresh if you don't need real-time updates when the app isn't open.
Turning this off for HeyPolo stops your location updating when the app is closed.

Algirdas bought two brand-new iPhone 16s, set them up the same way and switched off every service the test didn't need, including NFC. The only difference between the phones was that one had HeyPolo installed. Each phone logged its battery level every time it dropped by 10%, so the lines in the charts join those readings rather than recording every minute.

Phone Sitting Still
The HeyPolo phone used almost no extra battery while it wasn't moving. Both phones started at 90% on 25 July and sat on a desk for about a week. The HeyPolo phone reached 10% on the evening of 1 August, roughly half a day before the phone without it. Algirdas kept receiving updates and notifications from his group throughout, and they made no visible difference.
Road Trip
Driving is where HeyPolo used the most extra battery. Algirdas drove about 600 km around Lithuania over three days with both phones in a backpack in the car boot, so HeyPolo only ran in the background. When the phone without HeyPolo reached 60%, the HeyPolo phone was at about 23%. Most of the gap opened on the two biggest driving days, when the HeyPolo phone dropped about 10 points in 4 to 6 hours.
Algirdas's estimate from this test is that HeyPolo, like any app using GPS in the background, costs around 2% of battery per hour of travel. He's running more tests to firm that number up.
"My gut feeling is that HeyPolo, and any GPS application, is taking around 2% of the battery per hour additional. I need to do more measurements."
Algirdas Dumskis, Head of Technology, HeyPolo
Everyday Commute
Commuting is between the two. Over about six days of home → work → home trips, the HeyPolo phone was at about 16% when the phone without it reached 40%, which works out to roughly 1.4 times the battery use.
These are early results from one iPhone model, and each test ran once. Drain will differ on older phones, on Android, and when HeyPolo is open on screen. Algirdas plans to test the on-screen case next, and we'll add the results here.

Conclusion
Location sharing always uses some battery, and with HeyPolo most of it is used while you travel. In our tests, a phone sitting still barely noticed the app, while a phone on a road trip used about 2% extra per hour. Signal strength and your phone's settings change the numbers too.

FAQ
Is there a way to share location without draining battery?
Not completely, but you can keep drain low. Location sharing uses almost no extra battery while your phone is still, so most of the cost comes from travel. To reduce it, pick an app that stops using GPS when you stop moving, turn off Precise Location for apps that don't need it, and limit which apps can use your location in the background.
Will turning off location save battery?
Yes, noticeably so — particularly if you have several apps with "always on" location access. Turning off location entirely or restricting it to "while using" for most apps reduces the amount of background radio activity your phone handles. The impact is more significant on older devices or when your battery is already degraded.
Does turning on location on iPhone drain battery?
Enabling location services on iPhone does increase battery usage, but the degree depends on which apps have access and how frequently they're checking. The biggest drain comes from apps set to "Always" under Location Services — these run in the background continuously. Reviewing your per-app location settings under Privacy & Security is the most targeted way to reduce the impact without turning location off entirely.
Does HeyPolo drain battery when my phone isn't moving?
Very little. In our test, an iPhone 16 with HeyPolo left still for a week used less than 1% more battery per day than an identical phone without it, because HeyPolo stops using GPS when you stop moving.
Does "Always Allow" location use more battery than "While Using the App"?
Yes, because Always Allow lets an app use GPS in the background. While Using the App blocks background location, which saves battery, but location-sharing apps like HeyPolo can then only update your location while the app is open on screen.
Does running two location apps at once drain battery twice as fast?
Probably not twice as fast, but it does use more. Your phone limits how often apps get a location and can share one GPS reading between them, but each app still sends its own data, and sending data uses battery.
