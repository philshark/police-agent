Creating A Family Emergency Plan Built on Your Phone and Paper
A household that reviews a printed page twice a year and keeps a shared group running in between is not preparing for disaster so much as making sure nobody has to guess.
Phil
12 min read•August 19, 2026
The first time an alert went off on my nightstand at 1:40 a.m., it was a fire warning. We had decided earlier to stay and fight as we were well prepared, but as that slight sense of relief set in, I started to think about communicating that decision with my family in the city.
I grabbed the phone, squinted at 11% battery, and spent the next four minutes trying to remember whether my mum's landline number was saved anywhere other than in that phone. That's when I realised that it wasn't.
A family emergency plan works best when it exists in two places at once, because the pocket version fails in exactly the conditions that make you need it.
Batteries drain. Towers get congested. Cloud storage needs a connection you may not have.
Your phone is still the fastest thing you own for warnings and check-ins, and none of that changes the fact that a printed sheet in a kitchen drawer works at 3 a.m. with the power out.
Build both, keep them saying the same thing, and you have something your household can actually use when a night goes sideways.
FEMA's planning guidance covers alerts, communication, evacuation or shelter, supplies, records, care for household members who need extra support, and recovery. That is a lot of ground for one afternoon. Taken in order, with a pen and a phone in front of you, it is a Saturday.
Key takeaways
Wireless Emergency Alerts reach compatible phones through FEMA's IPAWS system and can be switched on and off in notification settings.
Name one out-of-state contact and put that number in every phone and on a wallet card for each person.
CDC advises storing at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days, with two weeks as the goal.
Write meeting places as exact street addresses, since a description means nothing to someone helping a lost child.
Generators belong outside only, at least 20 feet from homes, windows, doors, and vents.
Dialing 211 connects households to local shelter, food, legal aid, and counseling after an event.
Twice-yearly reviews catch the changes that break a plan quietly, like a new school or a changed phone number.
Map your real risks and set your phone to warn you
Start by naming the two or three hazards most likely where you actually live, not the full national catalog.
A household in Tulsa plans for tornadoes.
A household in Miami plans for hurricanes and flooding.
A household in South East Australia plans for fires.
Each hazard gets one protective action, written down in a single line:
Tornado. The National Weather Service names the interior part of a basement as the safest place at home, or "an inside room, without windows, on the lowest floor." Write which room in your house that is, by name.
Earthquake. The U.S. Geological Survey advises people indoors to get under a desk or table and hang on to it, the Drop, Cover, and Hold On protocol.
Hurricane. Find out whether your address sits in an evacuation zone, and write the zone letter or number on the paper copy.
Flood. Ready.gov advises learning and practicing evacuation routes, and never walking, swimming, or driving through flood water. Higher ground, and the car stays out of it.
Chemical incident. The CDC's shelter-in-place instruction is to seal all windows, doors, and air vents with thick plastic sheeting and duct tape. Pick the room in advance and note which one has the fewest openings.
With the hazards named, figure out how you find out.
Wireless Emergency Alerts are geo-targeted official messages sent to compatible phones through FEMA's Integrated Public Alert and Warning System, or IPAWS. The Federal Communications Commission describes them as text-like messages warning of imminent threats.
Check that those alerts are switched on in your phone's notification settings, because plenty of people turned them off years ago after a test and forgot.
Add the FEMA App, which sends real-time weather and emergency alerts from the National Weather Service and shows open shelters. Then consider a NOAA Weather Radio for the house, which can be programmed to alarm only for your county and only for the threats you select.
On the paper copy, this section is four lines. Hazard, protective action, where the alert comes from, and who in the household is responsible for checking the radio batteries.
Build your hybrid family emergency plan for communication and check-ins
Every household needs rules for who contacts whom, in what order, and by what method.
Without those rules, five people call each other simultaneously on a congested network and nobody reaches anyone.
Name one out-of-state contact. Local lines jam first, and a relative two states away is often reachable when your neighbor is not.
That number goes into every phone in the household and onto a wallet card for each person, including the kids who do not carry a phone yet.
Agree on texting as the default after a disaster, unless someone is in immediate danger. Text messages may get through more reliably than calls when networks are stressed. A text also queues and delivers later, which a dropped call does not.
Ready.gov's fillable communication plan form covers the fields worth copying: household roster, work and school numbers, childcare contacts, and meeting places. Simply put, this is a form you complete and email to yourself as a PDF. Print one copy and put a photo of it in your phone.
TPhone calls and texts cover most of it, but not everyone will be reachable the moment something happens. Set meeting places your kids can find and your parents can drive to
Pick two spots. One close to home, like a specific neighbor's porch or the mailbox at the end of the driveway, for a house fire where everyone gets out separately. One regional, like a library, office or a place of worship in a different part of town, for the days a whole neighborhood is told to leave. Convenience stores and gas stations don't make good meeting points, because people will scrable for food and fuel in the lead up to and in the wake of a disaster.
Write the exact street addresses, not descriptions. "Grandma's" means nothing to a police officer helping your nine-year-old. Put the addresses on paper, drop pins in shared maps on the phones, and say them out loud to the kids in words they will repeat back. The same problem turns up on a drive, where stopping on a rural road often leaves you with no address to give at all.
FEMA's post-disaster reunification framework for children uses family templates with dedicated sections for meeting places and evacuation locations, which tells you those two fields belong on the page rather than in someone's memory.
Ready.gov's materials for kids teach routes through mapping activities and a two-exits exercise, and children remember a route they have walked far better than one they have been told about. That practice is also what decides when kids go out alone on an ordinary day.
Households that split across two addresses need one more line. When kids move between a co-parent's house and yours, both households hold the same paper copy, with the same meeting places and the same out-of-state contact.
Two plans that disagree are worse than one plan nobody printed. A location-sharing app switched on for a set window, like the handover drive on a Friday evening, covers the stretch where a child is between two homes and neither adult can see them.
The car itself is usually already sending some of that same information, speed, route, timing, back to the manufacturer on its own, a separate flow worth understanding as vehicle data transmission before you assume your phone is the only thing tracking the drive.
Decide what makes you leave and what makes you stay
Write your triggers as sentences with a place and a route in them. "If the county issues a mandatory evacuation for Zone B, we take Route 9 north to my sister's in Jonestown" beats "we'll leave if it gets bad."
The second version turns into an argument in a driveway at 6 a.m.
Reasons to go usually include hurricane evacuation orders, fast-rising water, wildfire smoke, and a gas leak. Reasons to stay inside include severe thunderstorms and certain chemical releases, where the CDC's sealed-room guidance applies and going outside puts you in the plume.
Identify at least two exit routes from your neighborhood, because the obvious one floods or backs up.
Inside the house, the fire plan is separate and more urgent.
The National Fire Protection Association points out that a fire can leave as little as one or two minutes to escape safely once the smoke alarm sounds, and recommends practicing your escape plan twice a year. Draw two ways out of every room, mark the outside meeting spot, and tape the diagram somewhere the kids see it rather than filing it.
Ready.gov's flood guidance asks households to practice evacuation routes to higher ground rather than only reading them, and hurricane planning guidance from the National Weather Service asks you to know your evacuation zone before the season starts. Both of those happen on a calm weekend or not at all.
Stock supplies that match your routes, your outlets, and your pantry
Build in two tiers. The home kit aims for up to two weeks of essentials where you have the space. The go-bag covers at least three days and lives near the door you would actually leave through.
Water sets the floor. The CDC advises storing at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days, and to try to store a 2-week supply if possible. For a family of four, three days is 12 gallons, which is two cases of bottled water and a shelf.
Food, tools, and power fill in around it:
Nonperishable food and a manual can opener that is not the electric one on the counter
A first-aid kit stocked to the Red Cross list, which includes adhesive bandages, antibiotic ointment packets, and an emergency blanket
Routine medicines, copies of prescriptions, spare glasses, hearing-aid batteries
Flashlights or headlamps, spare batteries, and power banks sized for phones and any assistive equipment
A battery or hand-crank radio, dust masks, work gloves, sturdy shoes, a whistle
Plastic sheeting and duct tape for the shelter room, simple hand tools, cash in small bills
A waterproof pouch for IDs and insurance documents
Two rules matter more than the rest of the list during an outage.
On food, the FDA notes that a refrigerator keeps food cold for about 4 hours if it is unopened, and a full freezer holds for about 48 hours.
On generators, the CDC states that generators must always be used outside, never inside, and recommends placing them a minimum of 20 feet from homes, windows, doors, and vents. Carbon monoxide from a generator in a garage kills people every storm season, including people who left the garage door open.
Don't forget the people and animals who need more than a go-bag
Make a short roster of everyone in the household whose needs change the plan.
Disabilities
Mobility or sensory needs
Chronic illness
Infants, pregnancy
Medically complex children
An ageing parent who lives alone
Pets and service animals.
Then write what each one adds.
The CDC's disability preparedness guidance calls for batteries and portable chargers for adaptive equipment, plus at least one week's supply of all prescription medications.
Backup power for a medical device needs testing before you need it, and a written medication and allergy list travels better than a memory under stress.
For infants and pregnant household members, remember the feeding supplies, diapering, and medications, with planning that accounts for limited water, sanitation, and power. Formula preparation without safe tap water is the detail that catches people out, so store what you would need to prepare a feed with bottled water only.
Pets come with a flat rule. The CDC states that if it is not safe for you to stay home, it is not safe for your pet, and recommends identifying pet-friendly destinations in advance and assembling a pet go-kit with food, water, carrier, leash, and vaccination records. Shelters that turn animals away have turned families away with them.
For an ageing parent in another town, the useful setting is the low-effort one. HeyPolo has general-area sharing and time-boxed trip sharing, so a parent driving to a clinic can switch it on for the drive and off when they get home.

Protect your documents, your money, and your recovery path
Recovery is where the paper half of the plan earns everything. FEMA's Emergency Financial First Aid Kit is designed to help individuals and families at all income levels collect and secure the documentation they would need to get on the road to recovery.
Work through its worksheets once, keep one copy in a waterproof folder at home, and keep an encrypted digital copy you can open from your phone.
Add a home inventory.
Guidance published by the Oregon Division of Financial Regulation notes that creating a list when you're dealing with a tragedy is nearly impossible, and urges households to build the inventory every year. Photos of each room, receipts for the expensive items, and serial numbers on electronics take an hour and settle claims faster.
Know what an application will ask for before you are filling one out on a phone in a shelter parking lot. The DisasterAssistance.gov survivor checklist lists Social Security number, insurance information, damage information, and financial information as what applicants need to have ready.
One more number belongs on the paper copy. Dialing 211 connects you to a live, highly trained service professional who can point you toward local shelter, food, legal aid, and counseling in the weeks after an event.
The 211 line is the part of recovery most households discover three weeks late.
Turn the plan into a habit your household actually keeps
Pick two dates a year and put them in the calendar app with a reminder. The start of the school year and a mid-winter weekend work well because both already come with a household reset, and the school-year one folds into the back to school safety plan you are writing anyway.
On each of those dates, run a short home fire escape drill, drive or walk one evacuation route, send a family check-in text to make sure everyone still has the right numbers, and read the paper copy out loud for anything that has gone stale.
Moves, new schools, a new driver, a health change, a pet, a phone number that changed. A new school schedule moves more of those lines than people expect, since it changes who is where at 4 p.m.
Between those dates, test smoke alarms monthly and replace them at least every 10 years. Alarms have a date printed on the back, and most people have never looked.
Your phone can carry the maintenance load if you let it.
Calendar reminders for the drills, arrival alerts for the places that matter, and a low-battery alert that reads as a prompt to plug in before a storm rather than a reason to text someone.
None of that works if the phone's already dead by the time the storm alert lands, so it's worth knowing how location sharing affects your battery on an ordinary day, not just an emergency one. That last one is small, and it is the setting I would keep if I could only keep one.
The plan you build this month will not be the plan you need in three years, and that is fine. A household that reviews a printed page twice a year and keeps a shared group running in between is not preparing for disaster so much as making sure nobody has to guess.
Your kids know where to go. Your parent knows you will see the arrival alert without calling. The paper copy in the drawer says the same thing your phone does.

Frequently asked questions
How often should a family emergency plan be reviewed?
Twice a year is the standard cadence, and it pairs naturally with the twice-yearly home fire drill the National Fire Protection Association recommends. Households should also review after any move, school change, new driver, health change, or new phone number.
What should a family emergency plan actually include?
A family emergency plan should cover how everyone gets in touch if phones or cell towers are down, where you meet if you can't get home, who's the designated out-of-town contact, and where copies of key documents and medications are kept. Ready.gov's family communication plan guidance recommends identifying an out-of-town contact, since a call across state lines often connects when local lines are jammed.
Does a family emergency plan need to be written down, or is a conversation enough?
Written, and in two places. A conversation fades under stress, which is exactly when you need the plan. Keep a paper copy somewhere everyone can find it without a working phone, plus a digital copy your family can pull up from anywhere. The paper copy is the one that still works when the power's out and nobody can charge anything.
Who should be the emergency contact on a family emergency plan?
Someone outside your immediate area, ideally out of state. Ready.gov's guidance points to this specifically: a local disaster can knock out local phone lines while long-distance calls still go through. Everyone in the family, including kids old enough to use a phone, should know that contact's number by memory, not just have it saved.
What should go on the paper copy if space is limited?
The out-of-state contact's number, both meeting place addresses, the household roster with medications and allergies, insurance policy numbers, and 211. Those fit on one double-sided sheet.
What's the difference between a family emergency plan and a go-bag?
The plan is the decisions: where you meet, who you call, who's responsible for what. The go-bag is the supplies: water, medications, copies of documents, a phone charger. You can have a full go-bag and still not know where to meet if the house isn't safe to return to. Both matter, but the plan is what makes the go-bag useful under pressure.
Do text messages really work better than calls after a disaster?
Ready.gov materials advise that unless someone is in immediate danger, text messages may get through more reliably than calls after disasters, because texts use less network capacity and queue for later delivery.
How much water does a household need to store?
The CDC advises at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days, with a 2-week supply as the goal where storage space allows.
Should children have their own copy of the plan?
Yes, in wallet-card form. Ready.gov's family communication materials for children use wallet-size cards with the out-of-state contact and meeting place, which works for kids who do not yet carry a phone.
