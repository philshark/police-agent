You Share Your Location With Family. Who Else Is Seeing It?
Some of the most popular family safety apps have made millions from location data. Here's how it works, and how to check yours in two minutes.
By the HeyPolo Team · November 2026 · 4 min read
It's 8.30 AM. You open the app, see the little dot pull up at school, and breathe out. Your kid made it.
That dot feels private. It's just you, your family, and a map.
But for some apps, that dot is also a product. Every pickup, every doctor's appointment, every evening at a friend's house becomes a data point. And in the location data business, data points are sold.

How a "free" app pays its bills
Building a location app costs money: servers, maps, engineers. When the app is free, the money has to come from somewhere else. Often, that somewhere is your data.
There is a that buys, packages and resells people's movements. Its customers include advertisers, investors and, in some reported cases, government contractors.
Apps usually say the data is "de-identified." That sounds safe. In practice, it often means names and phone numbers are removed, while the precise coordinates and the phone's advertising ID stay in. Privacy researchers note that an advertising ID can follow you across apps and situations, which can make it a better identifier than a name ( ).
And a location history is hard to make anonymous. The place a phone sleeps every night is usually home. The place it spends weekdays is usually school or work.
The Life360 case
Life360 is one of the best-known family tracking apps. In December 2021, the nonprofit newsroom reported that it was selling data on kids' and families' whereabouts to roughly a dozen data brokers.
According to that investigation:
Buyers included brokers such as X-Mode, Cuebiq, SafeGraph and Allstate's Arity.
In 2020, Life360 made $16 million from selling location data, nearly 20% of its revenue that year, plus another $6 million from its Arity partnership.
Two former employees said the company did not fuzz, aggregate or reduce the precision of the data it sold.
Life360's CEO told The Markup that data is an important part of the business model that keeps the core app free.
A month later, Life360 said it would stop selling precise location data to most of those brokers. It kept selling precise data to Arity and aggregated data to Placer.ai ( ).
What its own policy says today
Life360's current is plain about it. It opens one section with:
“We may sell your sensitive personal data.”
It says the company licenses information, including precise geolocation, to select business partners for their own advertising and monetization. The notice also says Life360 does not do this with data from anyone it knows is under 18.
It stays on unless you opt out in the app's Privacy & Security settings or block tracking at the phone level. In 2023 alone, Life360 received about 1.47 million verified requests to opt out of the sale of personal information, according to the same notice.
Advertising is now a growth plan
Life360 is building a full advertising business. In its , the company said it acquired Fantix to launch location-based "Place Ads." In January 2026 it closed the purchase of Nativo, which it describes as a full-stack advertising platform.
In its and , the company calls its first-party location data central to that opportunity. It also says advertising revenue could one day rival subscriptions.
Why it matters once the data leaves the app
Once location data is sold, the app that collected it no longer controls where it goes next.
Take X-Mode, one of the brokers The Markup named as a Life360 buyer in 2021. In January 2024, the banned X-Mode and its successor Outlogic from selling sensitive location data. It was the FTC's first settlement of its kind with a data broker.
The FTC said:
The raw data X-Mode sold was tied to mobile advertising IDs and was not anonymized. It could match a phone to the places it visited.
That data could reveal visits to medical and reproductive health clinics, places of worship and domestic abuse shelters.
Until May 2023, the company had no policy to remove sensitive locations from the data it sold.
It sold location data to hundreds of clients, from real estate and finance to government contractors.
The FTC's case did not claim Life360's data was part of any specific harm. But it shows how far a location trail can travel, and what it can say about a family: where you live, where the kids go to school, which doctor you see, where you spend Sunday mornings.
3 questions to ask about your family's tracking app
How does it make money? If it's free and there's no clear subscription, your data may be paying the bill.
What does the privacy policy say about "selling," "sharing" or "licensing" location? Search the policy for those three words. "De-identified" doesn't mean untraceable.
Is sharing on by default? Look in the app's privacy settings for a "Do Not Sell or Share" switch. If you have to find it to turn it off, it was on.
