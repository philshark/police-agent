How Many Passengers Can a New Driver Have by State
Legal permission and readiness aren't the same question. Your state's rule depends on four things: the license stage, how long they've held it, passenger ages, and exceptions your state writes.
Phil
9 min read•September 15, 2026
The keys were on the kitchen counter, next to a phone lighting up with a message asking whether two friends could come along.
I stood there doing arithmetic I had no business doing.
The license in my kid's wallet came with no card explaining how many passengers can a new driver have.
The answer is (unfortunately) buried in state law, and it changes depending on which license your teen holds and how long they have held it.
Your state writes down legal limits. Whether your teen is ready is your own decision.
Most want the number first. Get the number, then get the rest of the rule, because the number alone has misled plenty of families into an accidental violation.
How many passengers can a new driver have
No single nationwide passenger number applies to in the United States.
Your state's limit comes out of four things read together: the license stage,  , the ages of the passengers, and the state's written exceptions.
License stage sets the baseline, since a learner's permit and a provisional license carry different rules in most states.
Time held matters because passenger limits often relax after a set number of months. Passenger age matters because states count only passengers under a stated age, not every body in the car. Exceptions come last, and they are where families most often get caught out, since immediate family and a supervising adult are treated differently from state to state.
Adult first-time drivers sit in a different position. Many state passenger restrictions apply to drivers below a stated age or to anyone in a provisional stage. A 27-year-old who just passed the test may face no limit at all.
Graduated licensing decides which rule your teen is under
Graduated licensing is the staged system that moves a young driver from supervised practice to full privileges. As of November 2021, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration reported that all states and the District of Columbia had a in place.
The first phase is the learner's permit, where a licensed adult has to be in the car. The second phase goes by different names (intermediate in some states, provisional in others), and it is the one that carries passenger caps and nighttime limits for most teens. The third phase is the full license, where those restrictions drop away.
Nearly every parent who calls a rule wrong has read the wrong row. A 16-year-old three months into a provisional license and a 17-year-old a full year in can share a house and a car, and still answer to two different passenger limits.
The legal age to drive with passengers depends on who is riding
The legal number in your state counts a specific group of people, not everyone with a seat belt on. States set that threshold differently, and the supplied state material shows cutoffs at under 18, under 20, and under 21 depending on the jurisdiction. A 19-year-old cousin in the back seat may count in one state and not in the next.
Exceptions vary the same way. Many states exempt immediate family, but each state writes its own definition.
A stepsibling or a cousin living in the house may or may not fall inside it. Adult-supervision exceptions carry their own conditions. States differ on how old the supervising adult has to be, and on how long that adult must have held a full license. No universal definition of either exception exists across the country, which is why the official source column matters more than the passenger number.
A teen driver curfew can make an otherwise legal trip illegal
Your teen can meet the passenger rule exactly and still be barred from driving at that hour. NHTSA describes an intermediate license as one allowing unsupervised driving under certain restrictions, and says those "usually include or with teenage passengers." A new driver curfew and a passenger cap are separate conditions on the same license. Both have to clear before the trip is legal.
Cutoff times vary by state, and some states also write exceptions for work, school activities, or a religious event. The curfew column in the table and the official source beside it are where to settle this for your state, since no national hour applies.
The practical version for a Friday night: a legal 7 p.m. departure with one friend can turn into an illegal 11:40 p.m. drive home with the same friend, without anything else changing.

Why is teen crash risk higher with passengers
Passenger limits exist because the risk is measurable, not because lawmakers distrust teenagers. The CDC states that "the presence of teen or young adult passengers increases the crash risk of unsupervised teen drivers," and that "this risk increases with each additional teen or young adult passenger." The describes a graded effect, rising with each passenger added.
The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety puts numbers on the gradient. AAA Foundation research found that the risk of death in a crash for 16- and 17-year-old drivers increases by 44% when carrying one passenger younger than 21. Risk doubles with two passengers and quadruples with three or more, compared with driving alone. Those figures describe 16- and 17-year-old drivers carrying passengers younger than 21.
The  does not extend them to every new driver at every age.
The mechanism is ordinary. A driver six weeks into solo driving is still building hazard recognition. Judging following distance on a wet road takes real attention at that stage, the kind an experienced driver no longer has to spend. Conversation, a phone passed forward, and three people reacting to the same song all compete for attention that has not become automatic yet. None of that requires a badly behaved passenger.
Is my child ready to drive with passengers?
Your state tells you what your teen is allowed to do, but whether they are ready is your call
And those two answers do not have to match.
The timing argument is on your side if you want to go slowly. The CDC reports that crash risk is particularly high during the first months of licensure. The crash rate per mile driven is about 1.5 times as high for 16-year-old drivers as for 18-19-year-old drivers. Early independent driving is the stretch where a gradual family rule has a factual basis behind it, rather than being a preference you have to defend at dinner.
Readiness shows up in behavior you can watch rather than in a score.
Can your teen keep their eyes and their lane while a conversation is running on a route they know well?
Does a missed turn prompt a calm next-exit correction, or a reaction that betrays surprise?
Does the seat-belt and phone agreement hold when you're not watching?
And can your teen actually tell a friend when they're being distracting? (which is harder than it sounds at 16)

The plan that holds up is the one your teen helped write. Before the first trip with friends, families generally settle four things: who is riding, where they are going, when they expect to arrive, and what happens if the plan changes. Agreeing that a change of destination gets a message before the car moves saves the version where you find out at 11 p.m.

Teen driver passenger rules: common questions
Is it safe to let my 16 year old drive with friends in the car?
Legality comes first, since the state's passenger rule for that license stage sets the boundary. Beyond that, readiness depends on experience and observed behavior. The CDC reports that crash risk is particularly high during the first months of licensure, and that risk increases with each additional teen or young adult passenger. Many families therefore start with one passenger on a familiar route.
Do siblings count under new driver passenger rules?
Many states exempt immediate family from passenger limits. The definition differs by state, and sometimes turns on whether the relative lives in the same household. Parents should check the family exception column for their state and confirm it against the official source listed there.
What is the legal age to drive with passengers?
No single U.S. age applies. The answer depends on the license stage the driver holds, how long they have held it, the ages of the passengers, and the jurisdiction that issued the license. Some states count passengers under 18, others under 20 or under 21.
Can a newly licensed adult carry passengers?
Many passenger restrictions under graduated licensing apply to drivers below a stated age or in a provisional stage. An adult first-time driver often falls outside them. Adults should verify the official licensing rule in the state that issued the license rather than assuming the teen rules apply.
How does a new driver curfew affect passengers?
Meeting the passenger limit does not override a nighttime restriction. A driver within the passenger cap can still be in violation while on the road during restricted hours. Some states write exceptions for work or a school activity.
What is a new driver night curfew?
A new driver night curfew is a restricted period during which certain permit or provisional drivers may not drive unsupervised. The hours vary by jurisdiction, and some states attach exceptions for employment, school events, or emergencies.
Can a new driver take passengers across state lines?
The research supplied for this article does not establish one universal rule for interstate travel by provisional drivers. Families planning a trip across state lines should check the current official licensing authority in both states before the drive.
Why is teen crash risk higher with passengers?
Young passengers add conversation and peer interaction while a new driver is still building hazard-recognition skills. The CDC states that the presence of teen or young adult passengers increases the crash risk of unsupervised teen drivers. The AAA Foundation for Traffic Safety found that risk of death in a crash for 16- and 17-year-old drivers increases by 44% with one passenger younger than 21. Risk doubles with two and quadruples with three or more, compared with driving alone.
