Family safety
Parents draw the line: 59% would quit a location-tracking app if it sold their data
When it comes to family safety, location-sharing apps become the mainstream tool. According to a recent study by HeyPolo, a privacy-focused location-sharing app, 63% of parents use these apps.
However, if they knew the app was selling their data to third parties, 59% of parents would take immediate negative action, such as deleting the app or switching providers.
Saras
2 min read•July 9, 2026
"It is unacceptable that some providers treat families as a cheap commodity, making their privacy and sensitive data become a source of profit," said Edvinas Sersniovas, CEO at HeyPolo. “Families install these apps for safety, not to have their lives and personal information exploited by tech companies offering a 'free' product.”
A survey of over 500 parents revealed that families care about their safety as much as their privacy online, and the way collected data is handled is a key consideration when choosing a location-tracking app.
Results show that 28% of parents would delete the app entirely if the app sold their data to third parties, while 31% would actively look for an alternative with stronger privacy protections. Only a small percentage (9.9%) expect apps to monetize their data this way and would continue using the service without concern. Another 14.3% find it annoying but wouldn't switch, and 8% would need concrete proof of the ad-tracking before taking action. 59% of parents would take immediate negative action, such as deleting the app or switching providers.
According to Sersniovas, it is no surprise that most parents would not accept companies making profits from their location history, habits, and other data collected and sold through such apps. It's part of a bigger pattern: when safety becomes surveillance.
“Parents are motivated to monitor their children to ensure safety, but being tracked by corporations damages the feeling of security and trust. Some companies do not clearly disclose their privacy practices, and users may be unaware that their data is being sold. As a result, people who care about data handling practices are forced to examine companies' policies to ensure an app meets their needs and respects their data,” explained HeyPolo CEO. “People are often put into a difficult and time-consuming situation to make a firm decision about which app is the best option for them in terms of privacy and how the provider respects their data.”
Parents prefer continuous tracking, but protecting the relationship is key
When it comes to family location sharing habits, parents prefer constant surveillance over check-ins. Nearly half of parents (47%) use a continuous tracking app (such as Apple FindMy) that displays their child’s location at any time, whereas only 16% use a temporary location feature requiring the child to actively share it. If given the choice to design their family's ideal arrangement, a striking 64% of all parents said they would want continuous, anytime access. However, the study revealed that the app should not only provide continuous access to live location but also be able to meet both sides’ needs and preferences to allow parents to protect their relationship with their children.
HeyPolo's real-time location sharing updates every five seconds, so families get that constant reassurance without the app quietly cashing in on the data behind it. Continuous tracking does raise a fair follow-up question about battery drain too, worth checking before you turn it on full-time.
The primary reason for those who actively choose not to use continuous tracking, is a lack of necessity (41% simply don't feel the need), but a significant quarter (25%) avoid tracking specifically to protect their relationship with their child.
The same tension shows up at the other end of the family too, adult children staying connected with ageing parents face similar questions about what a tracking app does with their data.
“We must stop turning family daily life into a commodity where connection, movement, and habits are the profit. This industry needed disruption: the default model too often rewards surveillance, rather than building trust and helping families protect relationships with their kids. For this reason, HeyPolo’s approach is built to respect privacy without selling data, and give parents and kids equal control rather than making one side the product,” said Sersniovas.
