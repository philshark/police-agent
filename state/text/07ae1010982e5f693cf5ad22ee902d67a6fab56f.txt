Buying Your Kid's First Phone Without The Regret
Most parents who regret their kid’s first phone point to the same early decision. This guide helps you judge when your child is ready, what to spend, and how to set the phone up so you aren’t arguing about it for the next two years.
Sinead
12 min read•October 21, 2026
The call came from a number I didn’t know.
It was a woman from the dental surgery next to the bus stop, and my son was sitting in her waiting room, soaked, apologising to her.
The bus hadn’t come. He’d stood at the stop for forty minutes in the rain, because he had no way to tell me, and by the time he gave up and walked into the first building with a light on, I was home wondering why he was late.
He was fine, and she’d been kind. I spent the next evening reading about buying a kid’s first phone.
Most of the regret parents describe later comes from handing over one device that does two very different jobs. It's a way to reach each other, and it's also an open door to feeds, algorythms, an expanding social life and a few hundred interruptions a day.
Separate those two jobs and the decision gets a lot simpler. Keep them together and you'll spend the next two years negotiating with a rectangle.
Readiness for a kids first phone shows up in behavior before birthdays
Middle school usually forces the issue.
Pickup times change, activities spread across the week, and suddenly your child spends forty minutes a day somewhere you aren't. Pew Research Center reported in 2025 that 23% of U.S. parents of children 12 and under say their child has their own smartphone, and the  among parents of 11- and 12-year-olds.
By 11 or 12, more than half of parents have already said yes to their kid having a phone
Age on its own tells you very little. The American Academy of Pediatrics reported in 2025 that a typical first phone in the U.S. arrives around 11, based on more than 10,500 children. Plenty of 11-year-olds lose a water bottle every week, and plenty of 10-year-olds have never left a charger at a friend's house.
Watch two things instead.
The first is whether your child keeps track of a backpack, a house key and a charger without a reminder every day.
The second is how they handle a no.
A child who accepts a boundary without three rounds of negotiation will handle phone rules the same way, because phone rules are just more boundaries. Think about the last house key you handed over, and how long it lasted.
The same Pew survey found that 68% of parents think a child should be at least 12 before getting their own smartphone, so a lot of parents buying at 10 are going against their own instinct.
If that's you, you're in good company. After-school logistics don't care what anyone believes.
Practical tips for buying a kids first phone without overbuying
The pressure to spend $700 or more on a first phone usually comes with a line you've already heard: everyone else has one.
Pew found in 2025 that 81% of parents whose young child has a smartphone said being able to contact them was a major reason, but only 7% said it was so their child wouldn't feel left out.
In other words, the data shows that parents buy the phone so they can reach their kid, and a $700 flagship pays for a lot more than that.
Feel free to tell your kid that one
RAND reported in 2025 that among students who oppose in-class phone bans, 81% say their parents should be able to reach them when needed.
If the main job is calling, texting and sorting out pickups, buy for that job.
A refurbished phone two or three generations old does all of it, and will make you feel a lot better the first time it lands face-down on the concrete outside of school.
Comparing the best first phone for kids across three tiers
Almost every realistic option fits into one of three categories, and each one gives something up.
Basic feature phones make calls and send texts, hold a charge for days, and have no browser or app store. Managing contacts on them can be clumsy, so check what the handset allows before you buy.
Purpose-built kids phones, like Gabb, Bark and Troomi, run their own operating systems with controls that are hard to tamper with and no open browser. They're simpler to manage and harder to get around.
Mainstream smartphones, like an iPhone SE or a Samsung Galaxy A-series, give you the most flexibility through Apple Screen Time or Google Family Link, but you have to set all of it up yourself.
If you go with a smartphone, treat it as a training phone. Keep day one narrow and open up access over the months that follow.
Mobile data and Wi-Fi filters
Any filter you've set on your home router only works on devices connected to that router.
Your child walks out the front door, the phone drops off Wi-Fi onto mobile data, and the filters stop working. The phone carries on as normal, and the protection is gone somewhere between the driveway and the bus stop.
That matters, because seeing the wrong thing online is the biggest worry for parents who are holding off. Pew found in 2025 that 72% of those parents named it as a major reason.
Two things help.
Call your carrier and ask them to put age restrictions on the line itself, which filters adult content at the SIM, whatever network the phone is on.
You could also choose a capped or prepaid data plan over unlimited, since a 2 GB limit caps how much video a child can stream from a park bench. Neither one is airtight, but both keep working once your child leaves the house, which your router can't do.
Set up contacts, apps, and alerts before you give the box over
Unbox the phone yourself and spend twenty minutes on it before your child ever sees it. Every setting is easier to start with than to take back later.
Here's the order to try:
Add approved contacts and limit calls and texts to that list, so unknown numbers can't get through.
Require your passcode for every app download and in-app purchase.
Turn off every notification except calls and texts from approved contacts.
Show your child how to block and report in any app they do have, and have them practice once.
Save emergency contacts so they can be reached from the lock screen.
Notifications matter more than you'd think. Common Sense Media tracked a week of real phone use in 2023 and found the , with more than half getting them overnight.
Across a 16-hour day, that's about 15 interruptions an hour, before homework even starts. Cutting that back before day one is a lot easier than explaining later why the buzzing has to stop.
Gaming is where a lot of parents get caught out
Banning social media apps feels like the main defense, and games can often be forgotten about.
A child who isn't allowed Instagram can still be on Roblox after school, chatting with someone whose profile says they're 12.
Thorn's 2024 research found that 83% of 9- to 12-year-olds use Roblox and 39% use Discord, and both have chat.
In the same research, 33% of boys aged 9 to 12 reported an online sexual interaction, up 15 points from 2023, mostly being sent sexual messages or asked for images.
Thorn points out that one year of data doesn't make a trend and the group is small. Even so, open the chat settings in every game your child plays and treat them as seriously as an Instagram ban.

Charge the phone outside the bedroom
Bedtime settings get negotiated, extended and eventually worked around.
A physical rule holds up better. Every phone in the house, yours included, goes on charge in the kitchen or living room an hour before lights out and stays there until morning.
The research here is observational, so it shows a link and can't prove cause.
A 2025 study in Pediatrics by Barzilay and colleagues, covering 10,588 U.S. children,  to 1.62 times the odds of not getting enough sleep and 1.31 times the odds of depression, compared with children who didn't own one.
The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory found that nearly one in three adolescents use screens until midnight or later on a typical weekday. In a class of 30, that's about ten kids still on a screen at midnight on a school night. The advisory recommends keeping devices away for at least an hour before bed and through the night.
A preteen who wakes up to a buzz at 1 am loses sleep they can't get back, and they might like the break more than they'll admit.
Pew found in 2024 that 72% of U.S. teens say they often or sometimes feel peaceful without their phone. Your child may grumble about the kitchen counter and still sleep better for it.
Write an agreement that loosens as your child grows
A family phone agreement works best as something you both understand, without a list of penalties.
Cover the everyday stuff: school rules, meals, homework, overnight charging, and what happens when a group chat turns nasty.
Pew reported in 2024 that 38% of parents and 38% of teens argue about phone time at least sometimes, and plenty of those arguments start with "you never said I couldn't."
So say it, in writing.
Then build in the loosening.
The American Psychological Association's 2023 health advisory recommends adult oversight of social media in early adolescence, roughly ages 10 to 14, with more independence as kids learn to handle it.
Write that straight into the agreement, so six months of handling the phone well unlocks a new app, a later charging time or more contacts.
When your child can see what's coming, they have a reason to use the phone well and no reason to hide how they're using it.
The last piece matters more than any setting.
Thorn's 2024 research found that among minors who had an uncomfortable online interaction, 84% used a block or report tool, and only 36% told someone in real life.
In other words, most kids deal with it on their own and never tell anyone.
If your child thinks their phone disappears the moment they report a problem, they have every reason to keep quiet, and no control can fix silence.
So tell them before the box is even opened that telling you about something will never cost them the phone.
The phone you hand over in September will mostly get used for ordinary things, like running late, needing a ride or asking if dinner can wait. Keep it good at those things and narrow everywhere else, and it won't become the thing you argue about.
Location sharing your child switches on, arrival alerts that answer the pickup question before anyone types it, and an SOS that reaches everyone at once give you something to act on without making you the household's watcher.
None of it means nothing will go wrong. It means you'll both know sooner.
FAQ
What is the best age to get a child their first phone?
Most children get one between 10 and 12. The American Academy of Pediatrics reported in 2025 that the median first smartphone in a large U.S. group of children arrived at 11. Readiness for a kids first phone depends more on whether your child keeps track of their things and accepts boundaries than on their birthday.
What is the best first phone for kids starting middle school?
If the main need is staying in touch, a purpose-built kids phone or a basic feature phone handles calls and texts without the app clutter. If your child also needs school apps, the best first phone for kids is usually a refurbished entry-level smartphone with controls set up before you hand it over, which costs far less than a new flagship.
Should I get my kid a basic phone or a smartphone?
A basic phone or purpose-built kids phone makes sense if all your child needs is calls, texts and check-ins, because neither has a browser or an app store. An entry-level smartphone with strict controls is the better fit if your child needs school portals, transit apps or family tools that only run on iOS or Android.
Can parental controls monitor text messages on basic phones?
Not really. Basic feature phones usually don't support app-based monitoring or approved contact lists, so you'll need to check the settings on the handset and look at carrier billing records for numbers. If you want closer oversight of messages, a purpose-built kids phone or a standard smartphone gives you more options.
How do I stop my child using their phone in the bedroom at night?
Set up a charging spot in the kitchen or living room where every phone goes an hour before lights out. It holds up better than downtime settings, which can be extended or bypassed. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory recommends keeping devices out of bedrooms overnight.
Why is transparent location sharing better than hidden tracking apps?
A hidden app gives your child a reason to switch it off and a reason not to trust you. When sharing is open, your child can see what's shared and turn it off, and that kind of arrangement tends to last into the late teens and early twenties, long after secrecy would make sense.
