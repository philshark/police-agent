But the relief is real and short, and the fear comes back bigger the next morning. How to deal with school anxiety at home when bullying or performance is the trigger Start with what you say in the first sixty seconds, because that sets the tone for everything after. Here is a sequence that many families find easier than improvising at 7:40 a.m. Name it without arguing. "Your stomach really hurts. I believe you. Anxiety does that to bodies." Do not debate whether the pain is real. It is. Ask one specific question. "What is the hardest part of today?" You want the actual fear, the third-period presentation or the walk past the lockers, not a general "school." Slow the breathing before you plan anything. Four counts in, six counts out, six or seven rounds, done together. A longer exhale settles the alarm faster than a deep breath in. Write one coping line together. Something they would actually say. "I have gotten through hard mornings before and I can text Dad at lunch." Put it on a card in the front pocket of their bag. Solve the specific problem. If it is the presentation, ask what one change would make it survivable, going first rather than last, or reading from notes. If it is the lockers, plan a different route and a person to walk with. Agree the smallest step that still counts as going. Sometimes that is a full day. Sometimes it is homeroom plus one class, with a pickup time your child knows. Avoid two moves that feel helpful and are not. Blanket reassurance ("nothing bad will happen")&nbsp; gives your child nothing to hold when something does. Pressure to toughen up adds shame to fear, and a child carrying both tells you less next time. Build the ladder on paper. Pick the specific fear, then break the distance into steps your child helps rank: walk past the building on a Sunday, go in for fifteen minutes with you in the parking lot, stay for one class they like, add a harder class, then a full day. Attendance is the goal, and each rung stays where it is until it stops being frightening. The foundations underneath all of this are boring and they work. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that children aged 6 to 12 sleep 9 to 12 hours and teenagers aged 13 to 18 sleep 8 to 10 hours per 24 hours, and links shorter sleep to problems with attention, behavior, learning, and depression.&nbsp; Movement helps too.&nbsp; The CDC recommends 60 minutes or more of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity daily for ages 6 to 17, with brain-health benefits including better academic performance and fewer symptoms of depression. Give mornings fifteen minutes more slack than they strictly need, so a wobble does not turn into a missed bus. Rehearse the social parts out loud, badly, in the kitchen. What your child says if someone is unkind, kept short and unrewarding: "Not interested." How they ask a teacher for help without an audience, which usually means catching them at the end of class rather than raising a hand.&nbsp; And agree in advance how they can reach you during the day, one text at a set time, so it feels like backup and not a check on whether they cracked. A short toolkit on how to deal with school anxiety if you are the student reading this This part is for you, not your parent. Your body reacting like this does not mean you are weak or dramatic. If someone is treating you badly, or the pressure to get everything right has gotten enormous, fear is a reasonable response to a real situation. Four things you can try today. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. In for four, out for six. Do it six times, in class while sitting comfortably. Nobody will notice. Answer the thought, do not fight it. When your head says "everyone will laugh," try "some people might not care, and I have gotten through this before." You are aiming for realistic, not cheerful. Ask one adult, in specific terms. "There is a problem with some people in fourth period and I need help with it" works better than "I am fine." Pick the adult you actually like. A teacher, a counselor, a coach. Set up one check-in. Ask a parent to text you the same thing at the same time each day, or agree that you will text them after lunch. Knowing it is coming helps more than you would think. Quiet, high-achieving kids often go unnoticed for years because they never cause trouble.&nbsp; AACAP points out in its guide for families that anxious children are often compliant and eager to please, so their anxiety gets missed, and that catching it early prevents it worsening with age.&nbsp; Show this section to a parent or a teacher. Plans made together hold up better than plans made in secret. Working with the school on bullying, workload, and accommodations Go in assuming the staff want this fixed. Most of them do, and arriving convinced otherwise costs you the working relationship you are about to need. Report bullying in a specific order. Start with the classroom teacher if the incidents are in one room, and go to the counselor or an administrator if they span the day or involve safety. Put it in writing, even after a phone call, so there is a record with a date on it. Document as you go: date, time, location, who was involved, who witnessed it, what was said or done, and what the school did next. Screenshots for anything online. Vague reports get vague responses. A list with times and places gets a plan. Then follow up. Ask what will change, who is responsible for it, and when you will hear back. Put that date in your calendar and use it. On formal support, the U.S. Department of Education's Office for Civil Rights explains in a 2024 fact sheet that under Section 504, a student with an anxiety disorder can be a student with a disability if the anxiety substantially limits one or more major life activities, and that reasonable modifications must be individualized to the student.&nbsp; Learning and concentrating both count as major life activities. In practice, accommodations might look like extra time on tests, presenting to the teacher alone rather than the class, a pass to leave the room when panic rises, a named staff member as a first point of contact, or a modified workload during a hard stretch. A caveat that you may have picked up on - some kids may find a 504 plan or being given the &lsquo;special treatment&rsquo; absolutely mortifying.&nbsp; Being pulled out for tests, or having a pass nobody else has, marks them as different at exactly the age when being different in&rsquo;t good.&nbsp; The accommodations may still be worth it. Talk through the social cost with your child first, ask which parts they would actually use, and let them help write the plan. A written plan your child understood and shaped feels like support.&nbsp; When school anxiety becomes school refusal and how to help your child go back School refusal means intense, anxiety-driven distress about attending that results in missing part or all of the school day.&nbsp; It is not truancy, where a child skips and hides it, and it is not ordinary Monday reluctance.&nbsp; Cleveland Clinic is blunt on this point in its guidance for parents: school refusal is not laziness or defiance but insecurity and overwhelming anxiety, and if refusal or significant anxiety or physical symptoms last two weeks or longer, it is time to talk with a children's healthcare provider. The pull to keep them home just for today is strong, and I will not pretend otherwise. Your child stops crying within ten minutes, and the house gets calm. The cost lands tomorrow, when the alarm reads school as more dangerous than it did yesterday, and the shame of falling behind on work makes going back harder again. Increase time and demands only when the current step has stopped feeling frightening, which is usually two or three repetitions. Keep bullying on its own track. If your child is being actively harmed and the school has not moved, it is reasonable to push for stronger safety measures, in writing, alongside the attendance plan.&nbsp; Graded exposure works for fear. It does not fix a situation where someone keeps hurting your child. Anxiety at this level is common. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends screening for anxiety in children and adolescents aged 8 to 18, and reports that 7.8% of children and adolescents aged 3 to 17 had a current anxiety disorder in the 2018-2019 National Survey of Children's Health. Cognitive behavioral therapy with planned exposure is the first-line treatment, and the evidence behind it is strong. FAQ How do I tell if my kid's school anxiety is from bullying or from academic pressure? The two can look nearly identical from the outside: stomachaches before school, sudden reluctance to go, a drop in grades. Performance anxiety tends to spike around tests, projects, or report cards. Bullying-related anxiety tends to spike around specific times or places, like lunch, the bus, or a particular class. If your kid can't point to a moment that sets it off, that's worth paying attention to on its own. How do I talk to my kid about school anxiety without making them shut down? Ask about the day before you ask about the anxiety. "What happened at lunch" gets further than "are you being bullied." Kids who are struggling, whether it's a bully or a grade, tend to answer specific questions more easily than broad ones. And they answer better when it doesn't feel like an interrogation they have to survive. Should I contact the school if I think performance pressure is coming from a teacher? Yes, and sooner than most parents do. A conversation with the teacher, or the school counselor if that feels safer, usually clarifies whether the pressure is about your kid's actual performance or about how it's being communicated. Teachers aren't always aware of how a comment lands on a kid who's already anxious. How can I stay aware without hovering over every part of my kid's school day? You don't need to know every detail of the school day to know your kid is okay. Knowing they made it to school, made it home, and are where they said they'd be covers most of the actual worry, without turning into a running interrogation about who said what at lunch. That's a narrower kind of reassurance than most parents start with, and it's usually enough. Is school anxiety the same as school refusal? School anxiety describes distress about going to or being at school. School refusal describes what happens when that distress becomes intense enough to keep a child from attending part or all of the day. Some families and clinicians prefer the term emotionally based school avoidance, since school refusal implies a deliberate choice. Should a child be kept home when they say they feel too sick to go? Once a pediatrician has ruled out medical causes, keeping a child home tends to relieve the anxiety in the short term and strengthen the avoidance pattern over time. Many clinicians recommend keeping some school contact most days, even a partial day, while a graded return plan is built with the school. How long should parents wait before contacting a doctor? Cleveland Clinic advises that if school refusal, significant anxiety, or physical symptoms continue for two weeks or longer, parents should talk with a children's healthcare provider. Can a child with anxiety get formal accommodations at school? Under Section 504, a student with an anxiety disorder can be a student with a disability when the anxiety substantially limits one or more major life activities such as learning or concentrating, and any reasonable modifications must be individualized to that student. Does location sharing make an anxious child more anxious? It depends entirely on who controls it. Sharing that a child sets up themselves, for a defined window such as the walk to school, generally works as reassurance. Continuous monitoring the child did not agree to tends to read as distrust and can add to the pressure they are already under. &nbsp;
